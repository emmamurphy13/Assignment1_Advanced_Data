## Main Findings
The ROAD to Housing Act does not require local municipalities to change their zoning. It's strongest measures come from penalizing cities whose building improved less than others and rewarding top growers. The findings from these data sets suggest this may be a sensible approach. The strictness of zoning regulations did not show clear signs of impacting how many permits a municipality granted -- this was true across the country's regions. Cities with a growing population permitted more, even when they were highly regulated. The data does suggest, however, that the legislation's Build Now provision punishes cities who slowed down growth and rewards cities who are building more now but still not a lot. This could risk punishing cities for meeting demand.

**Zoning and building**
(Uses 2018 Wharton Residential Land Use Regulatory Index and Census Building Permits data)
1. The West has the strictest zoning regulations
- Almost 42% of towns in the West are highly regulated. That's 10 percentage points higher than the Northeast, the next highest.
- Five out of seven of Western metro areas are highly regulated.
2. The South gives the most permits per thousand people and the North gives the least.
- Mean permits per thousand given in each region.
    - South: 7.5
    - West: 5.8
    - Midwest: 3.4
    - Northeast:2.4
3. Lightly regulated places build the least.
- Lightly regulated towns gave the fewest permits in every region.
- The Northeast had the least fluctuation in permit granting across zoning strictness categories.
4. Towns that are growing build more and are more regulated.
- Growing towns permitted more than three times as much as non-growing towns.
- 19% of non-growing towns are highly regulated vs. 28% of growing towns.

**The ROAD to Housing Act**
(Census Building Permits and Census Population data)
The Build Now provision (Sec. 213 of Public Law 119-101) penalizes cities that build homes at a slower rate during the most recent 5 years than they did the 5 years before. If there rate falls below the median, they lose 10% of their Community Development Block Grant (CDBG).
(**Note!!!! This data uses permiting data instead of count of home built, therefore, it is not an accurate predition of which cities are at risk to lose funding.)
1. Every region is almost equally affected by this provision.
- Northeast, South, Midwest, and West are within 4 percentage points of having 50% of there towns be at risk of losing funding.
- The towns the have the highest scores now were building very little from 2016-2020 -- a median of only 0.5 permits per thousand people. The towns that have the lowest scores for building now, built more during 2016-2020 than the highest scoring towns are permitting now (3.1 permits per thousand people vs. 2.4).

**Limitations**
1. Wharton Survey
- The towns in the data set with strictness scores are only ones that answered the Wharton survey. That means there isn't a complete picture of every U.S. town.
- There is no regression in this analysis, so it shows relationships between variables and not causation or correlation.
2. My Road to Housing analysis
- **MAJOR** -- the real road to Housing Act measures growth in housing units, growth in housing units permitted. Because permitting data is esaily accessible and a reasonable subsitution I used it instead to get a rough idea.
- **Exemptions aren't applied** The act has provisions for cities whose housing and rental price is already low, has high vacancy, has no power over zoning or has had a recent diaster. Towns who have increased housing stock an average of 4% during the five years are also exempt.
- **CDBG** It is not confirmed that every city on this list takes this federal grant money, nor is it confirmed that the list is complete. This group of city serves as an approximation based off of a city's population-based eligibility.
